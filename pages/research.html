Research

Collaborative Research Areas

GibbonFamily facilitates multi-site, cross-species collaborative research that no single team could accomplish alone.

Flagship project

Research Area 1

Gibbon Cognition: Wild & Captive

Gibbons are profoundly understudied in cognitive research, despite being apes with rich social lives, complex vocal behaviour, and demanding ecological challenges. GibbonFamily's flagship initiative aims to change this.

We are building a cross-site consortium to address questions about gibbon cognitive abilities including problem solving, curiosity, and social learning using standardised paradigms applicable in both field and captive settings.

Problem solving

How do gibbons approach novel problems? Do individuals vary consistently, and are there species differences?

Curiosity

Curiosity-related behavior across contexts at field sites and in zoo settings.

Social learning

Do gibbons learn from conspecifics? How does pair-bonding shape the social transmission of information?

Physical cognition

Understanding of causal relationships and object properties in naturalistic contexts.

Why gibbons for cognition research?

Gibbons occupy a unique position in the primate family tree. They are apes, sharing a common ancestor with humans, chimpanzees, and orangutans, yet their social system and ecological niche differ profoundly from other apes. This makes them a natural comparative case for testing evolutionary hypotheses about the social and ecological drivers of cognition. They are also understudied precisely because of the challenges of field research, challenges GibbonFamily is designed to help overcome.

Research Area 2

Social Behaviour & Pair Bonds

Gibbons are often described as pair-living and monogamous, but the reality across species and populations is more nuanced. GibbonFamily aims to build a comparative picture of gibbon social relationships.

Through shared behavioural datasets, we aim to address questions about: variation in pair-bond strength across species and ecological contexts; the role of extra-pair relationships; parental investment and its correlates; and how social bonds function under different levels of predation, food availability, and habitat quality.

This work will draw on data from both wild populations and captive groups, allowing comparisons between naturalistic and managed environments.

Research Area 3

Ecology & Conservation

Most gibbon species are forest specialists with large home ranges and low population densities, making them especially vulnerable to habitat loss. GibbonFamily aims to connect researchers studying gibbon ecology to enable cross-site comparisons of habitat use, ranging patterns, and population dynamics.

Key questions include: how do gibbons respond to forest fragmentation? What are the minimum habitat requirements for viable populations? How do demographic parameters vary across the range of each species?

This work has direct conservation applications, informing protected area management, reintroduction programmes, and national recovery plans.

Research Area 4

Vocalisation & Communication

Gibbons produce some of the most complex vocalisations of any non-human primate, elaborate duet or non duet songs that serve functions ranging from territorial advertisement to pair-bond reinforcement. Song structure varies across species and populations, providing a window into social transmission, individual recognition, and communication evolution.

GibbonFamily plans to build a shared acoustic database, enabling cross-species and cross-population analyses of gibbon song. We are particularly interested in questions about individual variation, dialects, and the cognitive demands of song production and learning.

Get Involved

Propose or join a project

GibbonFamily is open to new collaborative projects proposed by network members. If you have an idea for a cross-site study, a shared methodology, or a collaborative analysis, we want to hear from you.

We are particularly interested in projects that: are feasible across multiple sites including both wild and captive settings; use methods adaptable to resource-limited field conditions; and address questions with conservation relevance.
